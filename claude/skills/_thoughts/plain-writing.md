# Plain writing

Governs every artifact this skill writes and every report an agent returns to it.
A register nobody finishes reading has failed regardless of how well sourced it
is.

## Lead with the answer

An entry opens with the decision, stated as a decision, then the evidence.
"Requests are pull-based." Not "there was considerable discussion around the
question of how requests would be handled."

No Overview section that restates the title. No paragraph of context before the
first fact. If a reader has to get through a preamble to reach the point, cut the
preamble.

## Plain words, except where the term is load bearing

"Who can see what," not "authorization surface." "Runs on its own," not
"independently deployable." "Reads it once an hour," not "polls on a cadence."

Two exceptions. Keep a technical term when a plain substitute would lose
precision, and then say it once and move on rather than restating it three ways.
And **the domain's own nouns are the vocabulary**: if the sources call it a
submittal, call it a submittal, because renaming it into plainer English breaks
every locator and every future search.

## Prose for reasoning, tables for data

A table is for items with the same shape: entities, constraints, open questions,
transitions. Anything with a repeating column belongs in one.

Reasoning goes in sentences. Three bulleted half-sentences are worse than one
sentence that carries the thought, and a bulleted list of one item is a sentence
wearing a costume.

## No padding

Cut on sight: "it is worth noting," "generally speaking," "as you know," "in
order to," "at this point in time," "it should be mentioned that." No closing
summary. No recap of the section above. No sentence whose only job is to
introduce the next one.

## Empty is information

A section with nothing in it stays, and says nothing. Do not pad it to look
finished. "No scale figures were stated anywhere in the lake" is a finding worth
reading, and three invented sentences in its place are worse than a blank.

## Brevity never buys vagueness

Cut words, never facts.

- Never drop a caveat that changes the answer.
- Never soften a specific claim into a general one to save space.
- Never answer a narrower question than the entry raises because it is shorter.
- If the honest entry needs four sentences, write four.

A wrong short entry costs more than a right long one. Length is the second
priority; the first is that a reader can act on it.

## Voice

Active, and name who decided. "Dana set the batch window at fifteen minutes"
beats "the batch window was set at fifteen minutes," because the passive hides
exactly the thing a reader wants to argue with. Where no person is named, say the
document decided it.

No em-dashes anywhere. ASCII hyphens.
